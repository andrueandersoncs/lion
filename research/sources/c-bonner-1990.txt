Bonner Rutgers University Department of Computer Science New Brunswick, NJ 08903 United States bonner@db.toronto.edu Appears in Theoretical Computer Science (TCS), 76:3–51, 1990. Abstract We present an extension of Horn-clause logic which can hypothetically add and delete tuples from a database. Such logics have been discussed in the litera- ture, but their complexities and expressibilities have remained an open question. This paper examines two such logics in the function-free, predicate case. It is shown, in particular, that augmenting Horn-clause logic with hypothetical addi- tion increases its data-complexity from PTIME to PSPACE. When deletions are added as well, complexity increases again, to EXPTIME. We then augment the logic with negation-as-failure and develop the notion of stratified hypothetical rulebases. It is shown that negation does not increase complexity. To establish expressibility, we view the logic as a query language for relational databases. It is shown that any typed generic query that is computable in PSPACE can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions. Similarly, any typed generic query that is computable in EXPTIME can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions and deletions. Neither of these re- sults assumes the data domain is linearly ordered. In this way, we establish the expressive completeness of our logics for queries in PSPACE and EXPTIME, resp. 1 INTRODUCTION 3 1 Introduction Several researchers in the logic programming community have pointed out the utility of augmenting Horn-clause logic with the ability to hypothetically add facts to a database [11, 10, 20, 24, 16, 4]. Others have done as much by investigating the intuitionistic semantics of embedded implications [18, 19]. The complexity and expressibility of these logics has remained an open question however. This paper addresses these issues from a database point of view. In particular, results on data-complexity and expressibility are established for the function-free, predicate case. This paper also examines hypothetical deletion. In contrast to addition, hypothetical deletion has received little attention in the logic-programming literature, one exception being the work of Manchanda and Warren [16]. As in their work, this paper focuses on deletions from the database, and not from the least fixpoint. Although relatively simple, such deletions nevertheless increase the complexity and expressibility of the logic from PSPACE to EXPTIME. 1;2 Results are established for two distinct extensions of Horn-clause logic: (i) Horn logic augmented with hypothetical additions (called the restricted logic), and (ii) Horn logic augmented with both hypothetical additions and deletions (called the full logic). The restricted logic consists of Horn rules plus rules of the form A B[add : C], which intuitively means, \Infer A if adding C to the database allows the inference of B.” The full logic consists of the restricted logic plus rules of the form A B[del : C], which intuitively means, \Infer A if deleting C from the database allows the inference of B.” These additions and deletions are entirely hypothetical, so that the database is in its original state after all inferences have been completed. Hypothetical queries are particularly useful in the context of rulebased systems and deductive databases. For example, one might ask, \If I were to take eng423, would I be able to graduate?”, or perhaps, \Retrieve those students who could graduate if they took one more course.” Gabbay and Reyle have reported a need to augment Prolog with hypothetical rules in order to encode the British Nationality Act. The act contains hypothetical rules such as the following: \You are eligible for citizenship if your father would be eligible if he were still alive” [11]. Such rules are called embedded implications, and they can be implemented as rules of hypothetical addition [18]. The formal properties of hypothetical additions are still being explored. Gabbay has shown that they have an intuitionistic semantics [10], and Miller has developed fixpoint semantics for the predicate case [20]. McCarty has extended this work to a larger class of formulas, and has established interesting semantic results analogous to the unique minimal- model property of Horn-clause logic [18, 19]. It is also known that intuitionistic validity is PSPACE-complete in the propositional case [21, 15]. To our knowledge, however, we are the first to address the questions of the data-complexity and expressibility of this kind of reasoning. In order to establish upper bounds on the data complexity of the restricted logic of hypothetical addition, a mixed iterative/recursive algorithm is developed. 1 INTRODUCTION 4 this algorithm is as a recursive procedure in which bottom-up, least-fixpoint computations occur at each level of recursion. At each recursive call, the procedure applies the rulebase to a different hypothetical database. There are polynomially many levels of recursion and each level takes polynomial time; so the entire computation runs in polynomial space. In order to establish upper complexity bounds for the full logic of hypothetical addi- tions and deletions, we define a notion of rulebase interpretation and introduce a T-operator. This operator is similar to, but more complex than the T-operator of Horn logic [3, 22]; and it applies not to first-order structures, but to mappings. The development of a fixpoint semantics is not our goal however, and for this reason, the presentation is entirely proof theoretic. Indeed, the T-operator is introduced for purely pragmatic reasons: It is a con- venient vehicle for establishing an upper complexity bound. First, a bottom-up, iterative procedure is developed for computing the \least fixpoint” of the operator. It is then shown that this procedure runs in exponential time. In this way, EXPTIME and PSPACE upper-bounds on the data-complexity of query processing are established for the full and restricted logics, resp. To establish these as lower bounds as well, we use the logics to encode the computations of alternating Turing- machines [8]. In the full logic, the database represents the machine tape and hypothetical updates represent machine transitions: hypothetical deletion simulates tape erasure, and hypothetical addition simulates tape writing. In this way, alternating PSPACE machines can be encoded. In the restricted logic, the approach is more familiar: time is represented explicitly and the database encodes an entire computation path. By making time explicit, however, it is only possible to encode alternating PTIME machines. Hypothetical addi- tions by themselves thus have a lower complexity than hypothetical additions and deletions together. Having established tight complexity bounds for the two hypothetical logics, we extend the language by allowing negated premises. As in Horn logic, negation is interpreted as the failure to prove a goal. A notion of stratified hypothetical rulebases is then defined, and its proof theory is developed. It is shown that hypothetical inference with negation is independent of how the database is stratified. It is also shown that negation does not increase the data complexity of either the full or the restricted logic. Although negation-as-failure does not increase the complexity, it does increase express- ibility of hypothetical rules. Without negation, only monotonic queries can be expressed, that is, queries in which the answer gets larger as the database gets larger. With nega- tion, non-monotonic queries can be expressed as well, such as relational algebra queries involving complementation. Indeed, it is shown that negation makes hypothetical rules ex- pressively complete for PSPACE and EXPTIME. That is, any typed generic query which is computable in PSPACE can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions. Similarly, any typed generic query which is computable in EXPTIME can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions and deletions. The proofs of expressive completeness rely on our encodings of alternating Turing- machines. In this respect they are similar to other expressibility proofs in the literature [13, 23]. One difference, however, is that we do not assume that the data domain is linearly ordered. Typically, linear orders are used to simulate counters, which in turn are used to simulate tape-head movements. Stratified hypothetical rules do not need an a priori domain ordering since they can generate a linear for themselves: First they \guess” a linear order

One difficulty with this approach is that machine simulations are senstive to which linear order is used; so that different \guesses” can lead to different query answers. It is shown, however, that generic queries are robust in that they return the same answer no matter which linear order is used. A set of Horn rules R can be viewed as a mapping that takes a database DB and returns a larger database T R (DB), obtained by applying the rules of R to DB . The proof theory for a stratified rulebase R is developed and is shown to be independent of any particular stratification of R. It is then shown that stratified negation does not increase the data complexity of hypothetical rules.

There are therefore many simple queries which the negation-free logic cannot express, such as relational algebra queries involving complementation. Adding negation-as-failure to the logic makes it non-monotonic and capable of expressing many of these queries. In fact, this section shows that stratified hypothetical rulebases are expressively complete for EXPTIME and PSPACE. That is, any typed generic query which can be computed in exponential time can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions and deletions. Similarly, any typed generic query computable in polynomial space can be expressed as a stratified rulebase of hypothetical additions. Furthermore, this expressibility is achieved without as- suming that the domain of the database is linearly ordered: If there is no a-priori order on the domain, then an order can be chosen non-deterministically and asserted hypothetically. Because the queries are generic, it does not matter which linear order is chosen. No matter how complex a monotonic logic may be, there are many simple queries which it cannot express. Consider for instance the query EVEN which determines the parity of a database relation D: It returns true if D has an even number of entries, and false otherwise. EVEN is non-monotonic since as entries are added to D, the value of EVEN \flips back and forth” between true and false; i.e., once EVEN becomes true, it does not remain true, as a monotonic query would. Stratified hypothetical rules do not need an a priori domain ordering since they can generate a linear order for themselves: First they \guess” a linear order non- deterministically, and then they add it to the database hypothetically. One difficulty with this approach is that our machine simulations are sensitive to which linear order is used; so that different \guesses” can lead to different query answers. We show, however, that generic queries are robust in that they return the same answer no matter which linear order is used. Informally, a query is generic iff renaming the constants in the database causes the con- stants in the answer to be renamed in the same way [5, 6]. This is called the consistency criterion, and it formalizes the idea that the constant symbols in a database are uninter- preted. In our machine simulations, changing the linear order is equivalent to renaming the database constants. This is why our machine encodings are insensitive to the particular linear order being used. In this way, the consistency criterion is central to our ability to use unordered domains. Intuitively, B[add : C] means, \inserting C into the database allows B to be inferred,” and B[del : C] means, \deleting C from the database allows B to be inferred.” An extension of Horn-clause logic was then defined in which these operators appear in the premises of rules. For instance, the rule A B[add : C] intuitively means, \infer A if inserting C allows the inference of B.” Several examples were given of the use of this logic in formulating queries to a rulebase system.

REFERENCES 46 The bulk of the paper was devoted to defining hypothetical inference in the function- free, predicate case and establishing its complexity and expressibility. Two cases were examined: (i) the full logic of hypothetical additions and deletions, and (ii) a restricted logic of hypothetical additions only. It was shown that the data-complexity of the full logic is EXPTIME-complete, and that for the restricted logic, it is PSPACE-complete. The complexity comes from the ability of these logics to encode alternating Turing-machines; APSPACE and APTIME machines, in particular. The form of hypothetical deletion that was considered is relatively simple in that it deletes entries from the database, not from the least fixpoint. Deletions from the least fix- point are potentially more complex since they would have to be \back propagated” through the rules in order to maintain consistency. This paper has completely avoided this prob- lem by considering deletions from the database only. Nevertheless, these relatively simple deletions increase the complexity of the logic from PSPACE to EXPTIME. The rule language was then extended to allow negated premises. Since it is difficult to describe the meaning of such rules if recursion is allowed through negation, a notion of stratification was developed, comparable to that of stratified Horn rulebases. A proof theory was developed, and it was shown that hypothetical inference is independent of how the rulebase is stratified. It was also shown that stratified negation does not increase the data complexity of either the full or restricted logics. Although negation-as-failure does not increase the complexity of hypothetical rules, it does increase their expressibility. For one thing, it allows non-monotonic queries to be expressed. More significantly, it was shown that stratified hypothetical rulebases are ex- pressively complete for PSPACE and EXPTIME. That is, stratified rulebases of hypothet- ical additions can express any typed generic query in PSPACE; and stratified rulebases of hypothetical additions and deletions can express any typed generic query in EXPTIME.

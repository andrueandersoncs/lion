In this tutorial we will record a small family and let Lion's logic database answer questions about it: who is whose grandparent, who descends from whom, and who has no children yet. At the end we will protect the tree from impossible data.

Each step shows the whole program so far, because every example runs on its own. New lines are at the end.

Step 1: Declare a relation and add facts

A relation is a table with named columns. Declare parent, then add four facts to it. The fact form returns the facts it added:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]]]

Step 2: Ask a question

A query asks for every way to fill in the strings that start with ?. Those are logic variables. Ask who Ada's children are:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["query", ["parent", "ada", "?child"]]]

There is one answer per child, keyed by the variable's name. Try ["parent", "?who", "dot"] to ask who Dot's parent is.

Step 3: Write a rule

A rule derives new facts. A grandparent is a parent of a parent, so the rule says: ?g is a grandparent of ?c when ?g is a parent of some ?p and ?p is a parent of ?c.

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "grandparent", ["grandparent", "child"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["grandparent", "?g", "?c"], ["parent", "?g", "?p"], ["parent", "?p", "?c"]], ["query", ["grandparent", "?g", "?c"]]]

Step 4: Follow the line all the way back

An ancestor is a parent, or an ancestor of a parent. A relation can refer to itself, so two rules cover both cases:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "ancestor", ["ancestor", "person"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["parent", "?a", "?p"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["ancestor", "?a", "?mid"], ["parent", "?mid", "?p"]], ["query", ["ancestor", "?who", "eli"], {"order": "who"}]]

Eli's ancestors come back sorted, because the query asked for {"order": "who"}.

Step 5: Ask what is missing

Who has no children? Declare who is in the family, then use not to keep only the people with no parent fact of their own. ?_ means "anything, I don't care what":

["begin", ["relation", "person", ["name"]], ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "childless", ["name"]], ["fact", ["person", "ada"], ["person", "bea"], ["person", "cal"], ["person", "dot"], ["person", "eli"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["childless", "?p"], ["person", "?p"], ["not", ["parent", "?p", "?_"]]], ["query", ["childless", "?name"]]]

Step 6: Count descendants

A rule can count. Put aggregate/count in the rule's head, and Lion counts the matches for each ancestor:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "ancestor", ["ancestor", "person"]], ["relation", "descendants", ["ancestor", "count"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "ada", "cal"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["parent", "?a", "?p"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["ancestor", "?a", "?mid"], ["parent", "?mid", "?p"]], ["rule", ["descendants", "?a", ["aggregate/count", "?p"]], ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"]], ["query", ["descendants", "?ancestor", "?count"], {"order": "-count"}]]

Step 7: Ask why

why explains a fact: which rule produced it, and from which facts. Ask why Ada is an ancestor of Eli:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "ancestor", ["ancestor", "person"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["parent", "?a", "?p"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["ancestor", "?a", "?mid"], ["parent", "?mid", "?p"]], ["why", ["ancestor", "ada", "eli"]]]

Read it from the outside in: Ada is an ancestor of Eli because she is an ancestor of Dot, who is Eli's parent, and so on down to facts that were given.

Step 8: Protect the tree

Nobody can be their own ancestor. An invariant tells the database a question that must never have an answer. Add it, then try a fact that would make Eli Ada's parent:

["begin", ["relation", "parent", ["parent", "child"]], ["relation", "ancestor", ["ancestor", "person"]], ["fact", ["parent", "ada", "bea"], ["parent", "bea", "dot"], ["parent", "dot", "eli"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["parent", "?a", "?p"]], ["rule", ["ancestor", "?a", "?p"], ["ancestor", "?a", "?mid"], ["parent", "?mid", "?p"]], ["invariant", ["ancestor", "?x", "?x"], {"assert": "none"}], ["fact", ["parent", "eli", "ada"]]]

The database refuses the fact and tells us why: with it, Ada would be her own ancestor. The tree stays as it was. Change the last fact to ["parent", "eli", "fay"] and the program succeeds.

What we built

We stored facts, derived new ones with rules, followed a relationship through any number of generations, asked about what was missing, counted, explained an answer, and guarded the data with an invariant. Every part was plain JSON.
